Why PDFs Get So Large

PDF file size is almost always driven by embedded images. A single scanned page at 300 DPI can be 1–3MB. Scan 20 pages and you have a 60MB file. Even digital PDFs with screenshots, charts, or photos balloon quickly.

There are three main causes of oversized PDFs:

  • High-resolution embedded images — scanners default to 300–600 DPI, far more than screens need
  • Unoptimized image encoding — images stored as raw bitmaps instead of compressed JPEG or WebP
  • Embedded fonts and metadata — fonts embedded at full weight add unnecessary bulk

PDF compression works by resampling images to screen resolution (72–150 DPI), re-encoding them with efficient compression, and stripping redundant metadata — all without changing how the document looks on screen.

Common Use Cases

Email Attachments

Gmail limits attachments to 25MB. Outlook caps at 20MB. If your PDF exceeds these limits, compression is the fastest fix. Medium compression typically brings a 50MB scanned document under 10MB — well within any email provider's limit.

Uploading to Portals and Forms

Government portals, job application sites, and legal filing systems often impose strict file size limits (2MB, 5MB, 10MB). Compressing your PDF before upload saves the frustrating "file too large" error.

Saving Storage Space

If you scan and archive documents regularly, uncompressed PDFs fill up cloud storage fast. Compressing your archive can reduce storage costs significantly — especially for businesses scanning hundreds of pages per month.

Faster Loading in Browsers

PDFs embedded on websites or shared via link load faster when smaller. A 5MB PDF loads in under a second on any connection. A 50MB PDF makes visitors wait — and many will leave.

Does Compression Reduce Quality?

For text-only PDFs: no visible difference whatsoever. Text is stored as vector data in PDFs, not images, so it stays perfectly sharp at any compression level.

For image-heavy PDFs: Medium compression is generally indistinguishable from the original on screen. High compression slightly reduces image sharpness — acceptable for everyday documents, not ideal for professional print files.

For scanned documents: Medium compression keeps text readable and signatures clear. High compression may soften fine details — use Medium if the document will be printed or archived long-term.
